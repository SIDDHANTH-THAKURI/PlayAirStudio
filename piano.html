How to play

Setting up

  • Air is the easy way in, and the default. Leave your screen where it is, mark out a keyboard-sized rectangle in the air in front of you, and play that. Nothing to reposition, and you can still see the screen — which you cannot if the lid is tilted down at the desk.
  • Desk gives you a real surface to feel, which is steadier once you are used to it. Tilt the screen down until the camera sees the desk and your hands resting on it; a lid at roughly 45° is the sweet spot. It reads a tap by watching your fingertip travel and then stop, so it wants to see that travel: almost edge-on and directly overhead both give it less to work with.
  • Switching between the two re-opens calibration, because the plane you marked in the air is not the one on your desk.
  • Mark out your playing area by tapping its four corners — far-left, far-right, near-right, near-left. Tap them, don't click them: the calibration has to be built from the same kind of point the instrument later measures, and a fingertip resting on the desk sits a centimetre or two above the desk's own surface. Marking the surface but playing with your fingertips puts every key out by up to two whole keys on a low camera. Clicking still works if tracking won't cooperate, and you can click any corner afterwards to nudge it.
  • Calibration is remembered. Redo it if you move the camera, change camera, or move the desk.
  • Using an external webcam? Pick it from the Camera list — a desk rig usually isn't the built-in one, and changing camera means re-marking the desk.

Playing

  • Tap the desk like keys, with your fingers. Notes fire the moment your fingertip stops against the surface — the same instant a real key would sound. A note needs the finger to reach for the desk; dropping your whole hand rigidly is treated as putting your hands down, not as playing, which is what keeps resting between phrases silent.
  • How hard you strike is how loud it is, and harder notes are brighter as well as louder, the way a real hammer excites more of the string.
  • Both hands play independently. With the register split on, your left hand sounds two octaves down, so the whole surface is a bass keyboard for it and a treble one for your right.
  • Resting your hands on the desk is silent — only a genuine strike speaks, and a hand simply put down flat has no reach of its own, so you can leave your hands down between phrases.
  • Damping decides how long notes last. Pedal lets every note ring out like a piano with the sustain pedal down. Follow finger drops the damper when you lift off, so how long you leave the finger down is how long the note lasts — which is what makes staccato playable.

If taps aren't registering

  • Check the track figure in the header. Below about 20 detections a second a tap can begin and end between two looks at your hands, and no amount of tuning recovers it. Closing other GPU-heavy tabs and enabling hardware acceleration is the fix.
  • Strike, don't press. The detector is looking for a finger being stopped by the surface, not for a finger arriving at it. A slow deliberate press is deliberately silent — that is what lets you rest your hands down.
  • Keep your fingers reasonably extended. A curled finger is ignored, which is what stops a fist or a wrist landing from playing a cluster.
  • If the keys look like they're in the wrong place, the calibration has drifted — mark the desk out again.
